# What a model sees

Week 9 --- guest lecture, Nina Pikseleva

---

## Two systems that already exist

- Elgammal, Kang & Den Leeuw (2017/2018) --- stroke-level classifier trained
  on 300 digitised drawings, mostly Picasso, Matisse and Schiele
- Sun et al. (2015) --- a stroke-based CNN trained on 180 Chinese ink-wash
  paintings by six named painters

Neither is a toy. Both are published, peer-reviewed, and built to do exactly
the job this course keeps asking a human eye to do.

---

{/* _class: centered */}

## The numbers are good

- stroke-level accuracy: 70--90% per system
- whole-drawing accuracy: above 80%
- Sun et al.'s ink-wash system: 95% precision, 93% recall
- Elgammal's system, tested against forgeries commissioned specifically to
  fool it: caught nearly all of them

---

## That precision is exactly the trap

A model like this only ever tells you what it was trained to recognise.

- Elgammal's training set: under twenty named early-20th-century
  draughtsmen, mostly Picasso, Matisse and Schiele
- Sun et al.'s: six named Chinese ink-wash painters, 180 paintings total

Neither set contains Tang Yin, or anything like the Suzhou pian economy this
course spends a semester on.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> A model that agrees with the connoisseurs is not yet interesting, and one
> that disagrees is not yet trustworthy.
>
> --- Nina Pikseleva

---

## What it inherits

Train a classifier on a "securely attributed" set and it inherits every
misattribution already sitting inside that set --- with total confidence,
and no way to say which of its answers rests on a shaky one.

It also can't explain itself in terms a seal reading or a colophon can be
weighed against.
