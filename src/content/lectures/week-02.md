Chinese has its own name for this discipline: jianding (鉴定), authentication,
one branch of a broader connoisseurship tradition running back through Ming
critics like Dong Qichang. Their method leaned on documents --- seals,
inscriptions, colophons, catalogue entries --- cross-checked against a
connoisseur's own accumulated memory of thousands of paintings actually seen
in person. It is old, serious, and has settled more attributions than it has
muddled.

The art historian James Cahill, writing decades later, drew a line through
that tradition: a "verbal" method, built on documents and provenance, against
a "visual" one, built on close stylistic reading of the painting itself. He
did not dismiss the verbal method --- he used it constantly --- but insisted
it was a supplement to visual analysis, never a substitute, because a
document can be forged as easily as a signature, and often was. His own
casework shows both failure modes: a colophon correctly confirming a
genuine work, and a spurious inscription that fooled a later owner into
misidentifying an anonymous scroll for two centuries.

This course teaches both halves, in the order Cahill implies: materials and
style first, so you have an independent visual reading before you ever look
at a seal or a colophon, and can notice when the two disagree.
