In his early thirties, within a decade of the exam scandal, Tang Yin took
the Buddhist name Liuru Jushi, "the Layman of the Six As-ifs" --- 六如, from
the closing lines of the Diamond Sutra: all conditioned things are as a
dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow, dew, lightning. It's usually read as
personal piety, a painter fresh off his scandal finding a philosophy that fit
his circumstances --- he lived another two decades under that name, not a
death-bed relabelling. Read
against everything this course has spent ten weeks on, it also reads as
uncomfortably on the nose: a painter whose posthumous identity is mostly
made of things that only look like what they claim to be, choosing, in his
own lifetime, a name that says exactly that about everything.

This week doesn't resolve that irony; it sits in it on purpose. If the
Diamond Sutra's point is that clinging to a fixed, essential "real" version
of anything is the actual error --- not the illusion, but the demand that
something stop being illusion-like and become solid --- then a field built
entirely around asking "but which one is the real one" is asking a question
its own subject's chosen name already answered. That doesn't make the
question worth abandoning. It makes it worth asking with your eyes open
about what kind of answer you're actually entitled to.

## Outline

- the Diamond Sutra's closing gatha, and what "as-if" is doing in each of
  its six images
- why Tang Yin chose this name specifically, and when, in his own
  biography
- what changes if "authentic" is treated as a useful working distinction
  rather than a fact waiting to be uncovered
- what this doesn't let you off the hook for: the final project still asks
  for a defended verdict, not a shrug
