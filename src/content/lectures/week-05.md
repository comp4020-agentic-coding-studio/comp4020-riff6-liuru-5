A signature is a single, repeatable gesture; a seal is a carved object with
its own physical history, and that history is exactly what makes it useful.
An artist's own seals wear, chip, and are sometimes recut over a career, so a
securely dated impression can be matched against a disputed one edge by edge.
Collectors added their own seals as they acquired a work, so a scroll that
has passed through famous hands --- Xiang Yuanbian, later the Qianlong
emperor --- often carries a visible chain of custody stamped directly onto
it. That same density of information is what makes seals worth forging: a
convincing seal, carved to match a genuine impression, does more work for a
fake than a convincing signature ever could, and Suzhou pian workshops carved
plenty of them.

Catching a forged seal is a matching problem: comparing stone-cut edges,
vermilion paste texture, and impression pressure against a securely dated
reference, the same evidence-first habit as week 4 applied to a different
object.
