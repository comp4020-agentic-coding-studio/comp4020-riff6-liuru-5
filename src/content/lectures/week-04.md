A painter's brushwork is often described as a signature nobody can forge,
because it supposedly encodes something as involuntary as handwriting. That
claim is weaker than it sounds: workshop training deliberately produced
students whose brushwork matched a master's closely enough to sell, and a
skilled later forger --- the subject of week 10 --- could match it closer
still. What brushwork actually offers is not proof against forgery but a
vocabulary: specific, describable habits (how a stroke starts and ends, how
pressure varies along it, how forms are built from strokes rather than
outlined and filled) that a competent argument can point to instead of
gesturing at a general "feel."

That vocabulary is this week's actual content, because it's the thing
students arrive without and the brushwork study and attribution note require. "The brushwork
looks right" is not an argument; "the terminal taper on these strokes
matches three securely attributed works and not this one" is the same
judgement, made checkable.

## Outline

- what "brushwork as evidence" actually claims, and where that claim breaks
- a working vocabulary: stroke starts and ends, pressure variation, how
  forms are built rather than outlined
- comparing a securely attributed painting against a disputed one, live
- the painted study and attribution note: due end of week 5, grounded in materials and/or
  brushwork
