Tang Yin (1470--1524) was already being forged in his own city while he was
alive to see it. Suzhou workshops turned out paintings signed with his name
and stamped with convincing copies of his seals for a market that wanted
"a Tang Yin" more than it wanted a Tang Yin, and that market never really
closed: the same workshops kept going for two centuries after his death,
under a label --- "Suzhou pian," Suzhou fakes --- broad enough to cover
everything from crude knock-offs to paintings good enough that they later
entered the Qing imperial collection as genuine.

That is the reason this course exists. Not because Tang Yin is an unusually
interesting painter --- he is a very good one, but so are a dozen of his
contemporaries --- but because his afterlife is an unusually complete case
study in how authorship gets contested, decided, and sometimes never
resolved. Everything the field has for deciding "who painted this" shows up
somewhere in his catalogue raisonné: contested seals, forged colophons,
paintings whose attribution has flipped more than once in print, and a
scholarly literature willing to argue in public about all of it.

## Outline

- the scale of the problem: what fraction of "Tang Yin" is actually Tang Yin,
  and why nobody agrees on a number
- what this course teaches instead of art history as usual: a set of
  evidence types, applied to a running set of disputed cases
- the shape of the semester: materials, style, seals and colophons build a
  toolkit; the market and a real case study test it; computation and the
  forger's own craft complicate it; the last two weeks ask what any of this
  is actually for
- what leaves week 1: the two piles of reproductions for the first seminar,
  and the question you'll be arguing against by week 4
